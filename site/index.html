Hand-built versus managed retrieval

I built the same research agent twice over a corpus of academic papers: once by hand, owning the chunking and the character offsets, and once delegated to a managed retrieval service. Same corpus, same 70 questions, same generating model. The question was never which one retrieves more — it was what you give up by delegating.

The model points, the code extracts and verifies. A citation doesn't rest on the model being honest: it rests on the chunk keeping its position, and on a literal check confirming it.

76 % recall@8, hand-built lane · 53 of 70
61 % recall@8, managed lane · 43 of 70
0 of 560 returned passages carry a character offset
0 of 5 gains captured by the hybrid that looked obvious

The setup

Two lanes, one yardstick

An agent that answers with citations makes an uncomfortable promise: that the quoted text actually exists in the source. The cheap way to keep it is to ask the model to copy the passage. The way that holds is to have the model point, and let code pull the text by its position and verify it.

That difference was already measured before this experiment: on the same corpus and the same model, copying scores 141/162 and pointing scores 180/180. Delegating the chunking to a managed service puts that architecture in doubt, because the chunk stops being ours.

Lane A — hand-built

Own chunker with character offsets, a dense index (multilingual embeddings) plus a lexical BM25 index, fused with reciprocal rank fusion. Every result knows where it starts and ends in the source document.

Lane B — managed

A Knowledge Base service that extracts the PDF, chunks, embeds, indexes and answers. It is never told how to chunk. It returns passages of text, not pointers.

The shared yardstick

13 documents, 70 questions labelled with their literal supporting evidence, a budget of 8 results. A hit means the expected evidence shows up within those 8.

A detail that shapes every number here

The corpus is in English and the questions are written in Spanish — this was built for Spanish-speaking readers querying English-language research. Queries are machine-translated into the corpus language before retrieval, which is why a multilingual embedding model is required rather than optional. Measured: without that translation step, the lexical index retrieves 2 of 70 and drags the fused ranking below the dense index on its own.

Forensic detail · why the comparison is not symmetric

In lane A a hit means "the chunk we built contains the evidence", checkable by position. In lane B it means "the passage the service returned contains it", checkable only by searching inside the returned text. The units differ, and publishing both numbers as though they measured the same thing would be exactly the rigged comparison this project exists to avoid. It gets declared and measured anyway, because it is the honest comparison available.

The corpus · 37 sources, 13 with full text

What the material is made of

The real bibliography of a thesis on LLM-assisted personnel selection, organised into nine thematic axes by its author. The axes aren't decoration: they are the partition the question set is built on. Of the 37 sources, 21 had an open-access PDF and 13 survived download and verification.

Access is not a property of the document

This table went through three versions, and the first two were wrong for the same reason. They said "no access" and "closed" — claims about the world, written from what a program with no signed-in session managed to fetch.

Both fell to someone checking a single source by eye. The first time, a conference record marked "no access" whose full article rendered on its own page. The second, one marked "closed" that JSTOR serves in full on a free account — ten articles a month. Measured: that same page returns 83 characters to an automated browser and the whole article to a person with a session.

🔑 Access is a relation, not an attribute: it depends on who is asking, with what session, from which institution, from which country. A pipeline without credentials sees a more closed world than the one that exists — and writing that down as the document's status publishes your own limitation as a fact about the world.

So the column now says only in the corpus or not ingested. Of the 24 not ingested, 3 were read in full by hand and 4 have a copy at a venue the identifier doesn't point to — university repositories, JSTOR, PubMed. The corpus could grow without paying for a subscription; by how much I don't know, and I'm not estimating it again without looking.

Nothing was re-ingested: changing the corpus would invalidate all 70 questions and every measurement here.

Forensic detail · the auditor was wrong twice, too

First version: it read only the main frame and classified a conference record as "abstract only" at 2,167 characters. The full article sat inside a cross-origin embedded frame. Second version: it entered the frames, but still asked the identifier — which points at the publisher and its paywall — instead of asking which other venues hold the work.

Three venues block automation with a bot check or a 403 to a browser without a session. No attempt was made to get around any of them. What was verified, was verified by reading it in an ordinary browser, like any reader would.

Two states, because only two things are verifiable from this pipeline: in the corpus = PDF downloaded, verified and chunked. not ingested = the text wasn't obtained by automated means. What a person can read goes in the notes, and only where it was checked by looking.

Forensic detail · how they were written and what bias they carry

Questions were written while reading passages of the document, never chunks: a question written while looking at a chunk inherits the chunker's boundaries and ends up measuring the chunker. Each one declares the exact text that answers it, and an automated gate verifies that text exists literally in the document and computes its position. What doesn't verify doesn't get in.

The bias that remains, stated plainly: the questions were generated by a different model than the one under evaluation, which softens model-grading-itself overfitting but not the bias of having written the question from its own answer. A second gate rejects any question that echoes its own evidence. That is a mitigation, not a cure, which is why it's said here.

Results

Delegating the whole pipeline costs 10 questions out of 70

And 27× the latency. But the headline number misleads: what follows separates the cost of delegating the search engine from the cost of losing the lexical leg, which turned out to be very different things.

hand-built managed control variant

Delegating the engine: 0

With our own chunking and only the search engine delegated, the result matches the exact index: 42 against 42. The managed engine loses nothing.

Losing the lexical leg: −11

That's the real cost of delegating. An identifier, a DOI or a proper noun has no semantic neighbourhood: dense search misses them and lexical search finds them.

Tying isn't doing the same thing

When recall tied at 42, the top eight results matched on only 39 of 70. Two systems with the same number retrieving different things.

The lever that does pay

Reranking 20 candidates down to 8 with the model takes the hand-built lane from 53 to 60 of 70. It costs 1.4 s and a fraction of a cent per query. No combination of lanes comes close.

The ceiling trap

The union of both lanes is 58 of 70. It buys nothing.

Each lane rescues questions the other misses, so combining them looks like money on the pavement. But "one of the two brought it" is what a selector that knew in advance which lane is right would achieve — and no such selector exists. A real system has to fit both lists into the same eight slots.

Move the split and recall recomputes from each question's real positions. It isn't a simulation: it's the measured data.

all to managedall to hand-built

Why it fails, in arithmetic: three of the five questions the managed lane rescues sit at positions 5 and 6 of its list, where no eight-slot split reaches them. And the hand-built lane has 10 hits living in slots 5 through 8 — exactly the ones sacrificed to make room. Giving up four slots kills ten to gain two.

Forensic detail · and 16 slots doesn't fix it either

If the problem were a lack of room, widening it would unblock it. It doesn't: at 16 slots the hand-built lane alone reaches 57 of 70 and the even split lands at 55. Two different budgets, the same sign. The rule that falls out: under a fixed result budget, going deeper in your own lane always beats splitting it with the managed one.

Stated limitation: the simulator doesn't deduplicate across lanes, so if both return the same passage they burn two slots. Fixing that would favour the hybrid — but it would need to recover between 2 and 5 questions to break even, and duplicates free up few slots. Not enough to flip the sign.

The finding

What you lose isn't recall. It's the pointer.

Of the 560 passages the managed lane returned across the whole measurement, 560 carry a page number and none carries the exact position in the document. The citation stops being a verifiable pointer and becomes an approximate match somewhere on page N.

And there's a second loss, a worse one

The returned text is no longer our text. The service extracts the PDF with its own extractor, and the same passage comes out differently from each. This is the real case from the first question in the set:

The same lane, measured two ways

Compared literally against our document, the managed lane appears to collapse to 5 of 70. The 38-question gap isn't missing evidence: it's line breaks. Literal measurement here isn't measuring retrieval — it's measuring the disagreement between two extractors.

The architectural consequence

With a managed service, this project's deterministic citation check cannot run as written: it forces you to normalise whitespace. And normalising is precisely what — measured separately — welds non-contiguous text together and lets something absent pass as present.

The managed lane doesn't degrade the citation. It removes the floor the method stands on.

Method

Five times the instrument failed, not the system

The part of this experiment that changed its mind most wasn't the result — it was the way of measuring it. Each of these started as a firm conclusion and ended refuted by its own measurement.

refuted

The best PDF extractor

Five rounds, seven candidates, twelve documents. The final conclusion is the opposite of round one's: the initial winner, with a spotless 33 out of 33, dropped to fourth place once an independent referee judged it instead of itself.

refuted

"The managed lane retrieves less"

First number: 5 of 70. I opened one concrete case and compared both texts character by character: identical apart from a line break. It was measuring extractor disagreement, not retrieval.

refuted

"More judges is better"

Of three automated judges, two survive. The third rejected 44 % of correct answers. A judge that doesn't earn its keep is worse than no judge.

refuted

"Translation is the lever"

It has 15 genuine defects out of 70 and recall is identical against a reference translation. Badly translated questions fail less (13 %) than flawless ones (27 %).

refuted

"The hybrid adds up"

The union was 58 of 70. The real hybrid captures 0 of those 5. An oracle ceiling is not a system.

recurring pattern

The default that reads as a result

A mutation that never applies leaves detection measured over zero cases, and 0/0 reads as 100 %. It showed up six times in this project — including once where a missing dependency was reported as "the service is down".

De-hyphenation belongs in the normalised view, never in the raw text: it lifts recovery from 57 % to 86 %, but applied to the raw text it corrupts the rest by joining legitimate hyphens. That validates, by measurement, the design of two representations with an index map between them.

Spec sheet

What got built, and what stays open

Guardrails

PDF-borne prompt injection and pseudonymisation at ingestion. External detection 8 of 8, with 1 false rejection in 1,263. Document-borne injection is real: a PDF off the internet can carry instructions a naive reader obeys.

Automated judges

Three built, two switched on. Support and attribution earn their keep; completeness doesn't. The attribution judge rests on 6 cases, and that gets declared rather than rounded away.

Observability

Per-query trace with sub-questions, scored chunks, cited chunks and rejections. US$ 0.000113 and about 3 s per query.

Verification

211 automated tests. An adversarial validation pass found 14 defects with all of them green, two of which broke the system's central promise.

What this experiment does not prove

  • The generating model isn't the one I'd have chosen: the provider had the main model family quota-blocked throughout. The judges run on the same model that generates, and that's the largest bias still standing.
  • Thirteen documents and seventy questions can separate lanes that differ by ten points, not claim differences of two.
  • Results hold for English-language academic papers queried in Spanish. A corpus of internal documents, with tables and forms, is a different problem.
  • The managed service's chunking was used at its default settings. Tuning it would likely move its recall; it would not move the loss of the pointer, which is structural.
